Audio Dramas and Welcome to Night Vale

Intro

Last semester, for one of my classes I chose to research why trans people so commonly take voice acting roles in audio dramas (which is actually very interesting!!). While researching this, I found that almost without fail, if a study did not mention the audio drama Welcome to Night Vale (WTNV), it was not a study that I could use in my research. Coincidence, maybe, but I figure I'd better start off my project this semester with my load-bearing WTNV mention just to be safe!

I'll start by giving an overview on WTNV, then move on to why WTNV is so important to audio dramas as a whole.

(Before we get started though, up top regarding audio dramas versus podcasts: 'podcast' is the term that came first, to refer to the audio-based medium where people tell stories. 'Audio drama' came later, to distinguish narrative voice acted+soundscaped shows from talk style podcasts. Every audio drama is a podcast, but not every podcast is an audio drama! In academic settings, I use audio drama for clarity, but for the most part they're interchangeable.)

Summary

Started in 2012 and still ongoing today, WTNV is widely considered to be extremely foundational in the audio drama community, alongside another groundbreaking audio drama started in 2014, Wolf 359. Both of these audio dramas began as no budget passion projects, using cheap microphones and free editing software to create (in my opinion) beautiful stories.

WTNV is styled after a community radio channel, with listeners tuning in to the Night Vale Community Radio (NVCR), hosted by the voice of Night Vale, Cecil Palmer. It is through Cecil that we learn about the desert town of Night Vale, dutifully kept updated on local news, traffic, and of course, the weather. However, we're quick to find that nothing is as we'd expect in this little desert community. I think that the opening segment sets the stage better than I could:

"Hello listeners. To start things off, I’ve been asked to read to read this brief notice. The City Council announces the opening of a new dog park at the corner of Earl and Summerset, near the Ralphs. They would like to remind everyone that dogs are not allowed in the dog park. People are not allowed in the dog park. It is possible you will see hooded figures in the dog park. Do not approach them. Do not approach the dog park. The fence is electrified and highly dangerous. Try not to look at the dog park and especially do not look for any period of time at the hooded figures. The dog park will not harm you."

The world of WTNV is beautifully strange in ways that are, in many cases, familiar and often satirical. The City Council is an unknowable and possibly a many-headed being (or perhaps a hivemind?) that leaves Night Vale with conveniently timed vacations the second there's any strife or danger. The Secret Police are the only police in Night Vale (except, of course, for the Secret Secret Police), and there is a Vague Yet Threatening Government Agency (and yes, that is all we know about it). There are "reliable and invasive spy satellites" scanning people's thoughts at all times. And god forbid you get an internship at NVCR - that's a position with a nearly 100% mortality rate. And worse than that, it's unpaid, and Cecil is terrible at completing the paperwork so you get your college credit.

And even despite all of this, Night Vale is undeniably known as being comforting, "safe." This is due in no small part to the unapologetic queerness of WTNV (more on why this is significant later). In episode one, Cecil declares his love of the scientist Carlos, and it only grows from there. I mean honestly, just read this:

"That new scientist, we now know he’s named Carlos, called a town meeting. He has a square jaw, and teeth like a military cemetery. His hair is perfect, and we all hate and despair and love that perfect hair in equal measure. [...] Carlos told us that we are, by far, the most scientifically interesting community in the US, and he had come to study just what is going on around here. He grinned, and everything about him was perfect, and I fell in love instantly. Government agents from a vague yet menacing agency were in the back, watching. I fear for Carlos. I fear for Night Vale. I fear for anyone caught between what they know and what they don’t yet know that they don’t know."

Why WTNV Matters

Though not exhaustive, here's a quick list of some things that cemented WTNV as a giant in audio drama spaces:

  • Voice acting and sound design - WTNV has full voice acting, soundscaping, and music! I would argue that Wolf 359 (mentioned earlier) pushes this even further, but together the two really set the bar for what audio dramas could be (as both of these are among the earliest audio dramas created - they set the standard).
  • Queer representation - the queer rep in WTNV is immediate, unflinching, and even more than that, treated as completely normal. There are no huge coming out scenes, no explanation, just a matter of fact presentation of the characters' lives, which happpens to include queerness. For the primary audience at the height of its popularity (young, queer, and often closeted people), and in 2012, this was huge. (Also notable is the autistic, disabled, and poc representation!)
  • Remember how earlier I mentioned that WTNV (and Wolf 359) were made with no budget? Audio dramas have an incredibly low barrier to entry, especially when compared with other mediums such as books or movies. This is the major factor for why there's so much representation in audio dramas - if you've got the ability to write a script, a microphone, and access to any audio editing program, you can make an audio drama. This was the crux of my entire research project last semester!
  • (There are other factors too, but the low barrier to entry 100% is the biggest one.)
  • Authenticity - while difficult at best to define and quantify what "sheer aura" is, it's easy to see that WTNV is absolutely dripping with it. WTNV never once flinches back from being wholly itself. The entire WTNV is earnest in their work on the show, and that passion bleeds through to the audience.
  • Passion and consistency - and here's the obligatory "they just kept doing it until it worked out" bullet point. WTNV has been consistently uploading (aside from some hiatuses, which are incredibly standard in most medias) since 2012. While not guaranteed by any means, sticking to something for that long usually results in some measure of success eventually. However, it's very clear that WTNV was never started to be "the next big thing" or anything like that. In fact, in bonus episodes released, Joseph Fink and Jeffrey Cranor (the writers) have talked about being surprised and even offput and anxious when WTNV started blowing up. Rather, they were writing and producing WTNV out of sheer passion. Connecting back to the previous bullet point, the love that the writers have for WTNV

All of these factors (in addition with others not mentioned here) combined to bring WTNV to incredible heights. WTNV not only established what the medium could be, but also proved how far it could go. It's a common refrain of audio drama creators that there is no money in podcasting, but WTNV was among the first to be the exception. Nowadays, there are other audio dramas that are also finding that exception (such as The Magnus Archives, The Penumbra Podcast, and Malevolent), but the vast majority still remain budgetless passion projects. It's those lesser known audio dramas that I really want to focus on and highlight in this project!

Conclusion

WTNV is an extremely impressive piece of media. The process of brainstorming a story, writing and editing scripts, scheduling actors, recording lines, sorting through takes, ordering the audio, trimming and editing it, soundscaping, scoring, and balancing levels is an incredible amount of work to do even once. As of September 17th, WTNV has done that process 294 times (plus organizing multiple live shows, and releasing multiple books!). It's an incredible labor of love and passion, which is what the audio drama community is built on. I was kind of joking around at the beginning about the load-bearing WTNV mention, but I do genuinely believe that, if you're going to talk about audio dramas in a meaningful way, you have to acknowledge how they started. It is my hope that this has provided you some of that history and understanding of why audio dramas are the way they are today.
